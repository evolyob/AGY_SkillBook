# How to Write the Golden Three Chapters: Web Novel Opening Checklist & Rewrite Examples

The Golden Three Chapters means looking at the first three chapters of a web novel together: can readers quickly see what situation the protagonist is in, what they need to accomplish right now, what makes this book worth reading, and why they should keep turning the pages? Start by pulling the reader into the drama with one concrete matter, then deliver tangible progress and payoff in each subsequent chapter. Word-count positions and numbers of thrill beats in Oh Story are only default references; specific execution adjusts to your genre and book's pacing rather than locking your writing to rigid numbers.

## Which Three Chapters Are the Golden Three Chapters, and What Must They Establish?

The Golden Three Chapters refer to the first three chapters of a serialized web novel; Oh Story treats them as a unit for checking whether an opening can stand on its own. Within these three chapters, you should make clear: who the protagonist is and what their situation is, what the first conflict is, what sets them apart, and why readers should keep reading.

This page covers openings for long-form serials; for short-story openings, see Short Story from Idea to Draft. In a long-form novel, the first conflict should be strongly tied to the main plot—once it is resolved, the main plot starts turning with it.

## What to Write in Each of the Golden Three Chapters: First-Three-Chapters Checklist

| Chapter | Checkpoints & Chapter Ending |
|---|---|
| Chapter 1 | **Check**: Who is in what situation, what they are trying to do, and what makes this book worth reading; **Ending**: A new discovery, choice, relationship shift, or emotional anticipation |
| Chapter 2 | **Check**: How the original goal advances, and how obstacles or understanding change; **Ending**: Partial payoff and unfinished business |
| Chapter 3 | **Check**: What stage of progress readers receive, and whether the long-term direction is clear; **Ending**: An action pointing toward the main plot or a lingering aftertaste |

Use this table to verify the division of labor across the first three chapters, adjusting the exact arrangement to your book's genre:

- The protagonist and their immediate task are clear, without piling up new terminology until readers cannot tell primary from secondary
- The opening trouble connects directly to the core hook/appeal of the whole book
- Background is brought out naturally as the plot requires, without overshadowing what is happening right now
- Stage-by-stage outcomes genuinely land, and everyone present reacts in line with their own interests
- The next chapter picks up smoothly on the goal left by the previous chapter
- The appetite whetted by the title and blurb matches the flavor delivered by the main text

Reference materials include fast-paced default baselines such as "protagonist appears within ~300 characters, expectations established within 1,000 characters"—when your book has explicit requirements, follow your book. Opening with weather, daily life, flashbacks, multiple characters appearing, or an ordinary person with no cheat/golden finger are all fine, as long as they pull readers into the immediate situation.

## How to Write a Web Novel Opening: Three Anchors—Character Setup, Entry Point, and Golden Finger

Oh Story's opening reference builds a commercial web novel opening around three items: character setup, entry point, and golden finger. When applying this to your own book, first clarify what your book promises to show readers; a story about ordinary human relationships and worldly affairs has no need for superpowers.

| Observation Item | Question & How to Put Pen to Paper |
|---|---|
| Character | **Ask first**: What do they care about, and what is their current situation? **Write**: Show a trade-off/choice, asking for help, or on-the-spot observation |
| Entry Point | **Ask first**: Through what event do you bring readers into the book? **Write**: A conflict, an opportunity, a relationship, or flavorful everyday life |
| Advantage | **Ask first**: If the book features a unique ability, what is it? **Write**: Demonstrate one bounded use of it; skip if not writing special abilities |

Opening from a predicament first lets readers clearly see where the protagonist's difficulty lies, then watch how they break through; opening from an advantage first shows off a genuine skill, then uses a thorny assignment to test its depth. Either opening works—there is no requirement that the first three chapters must involve humiliation, a comeback/reversal, or drawing a crowd of onlookers.

If you write winning a round in public, make the reactions of those present and the benefits gained concrete; if you write private growth or a warming relationship, a word of recognition, a bit of tacit understanding, or reaching a cooperation also counts as payoff. Worldbuilding background is introduced in batches alongside the current plot; atmospheric genres may also lay out the environment first.

## Golden Three Chapters Example: Rewriting a Background-First Opening into a Conflict-First Opening

Original setting for this page: Apprentice Xu Qinghe borrows Zhou Jian's shopfront and repair bench, with the agreement expiring at the end of the month. Zhou Jian plans to sell all of his equipment; the watches left for repair still belong to customers. Xu Qinghe wants to finish the orders she has already accepted and find a place to keep repairing watches before month-end. When she turns back the minute hand of a stopped watch, she can see a snippet of the wearer's field of vision right before the watch stopped—giving only fragments, without dates, the full picture, or motives.

**A background-first approach** is not inherently wrong, but the passage below gets absorbed in reciting biographical details and introduces the immediate difficulty too late:

> There was a river in the county town. Xu Qinghe had learned watch repair from her master since childhood. After her master passed away, she continued working in the original shop. She had a responsible personality. At the end of the month, she had to move out.

**Version with the task placed upfront**:

> Zhou Jian pressed the equipment transfer list down onto the glass counter: "Clear out by month-end. Those seven customer repair watches you took in—can you finish delivering them?"
>
> "I'll deliver Boss Liu's first." Xu Qinghe pulled out the photo taken the day the watch was received, with the inspection record clipped beside it: water ingress was discovered on the spot when opening the case, before any repair had started. Boss Liu had agreed to pick it up at three in the afternoon.
>
> She turned the stopped minute hand backward. The ticking of the wall clock faded away, and before her eyes appeared a hand wearing a square ring, pressing the watch into muddy water. The fragment cut off there. She looked back at the wall clock: one fifty-one, sixty-nine minutes left until three.

The list sets the month-end deadline, the seven repair watches are the responsibility and task she must shoulder, and the photo plus inspection record nail down the water damage as occurring prior to repair. The square-ring fragment only prompts her to keep asking questions; it cannot yet prove who damaged the watch.

| Chapter | Design & Actual Delivery |
|---|---|
| Chapter 1 | Must relocate the shop by month-end; first handle Boss Liu's water-damaged gold watch; turning the hand only reveals the fragment of a square ring pressing the watch; at chapter end, Boss Liu arrives to pick up the watch |
| Chapter 2 | Boss Liu insists the water got in during repair; Xu Qinghe lays out the intake photo and inspection record; after checking them, he withdraws that claim and accepts the 80-yuan quote; the watch is repaired, inspected, and paid in full |
| Chapter 3 | Xu Qinghe sets that labor fee aside as relocation budget, contacts the rentable workstation mentioned earlier, and schedules a viewing; a new customer brings a stopped watch tied to her master's past |

The photo and record only show that the water damage preceded the repair; a single ring cannot pin full blame on anyone. Boss Liu withdrawing his claim and pulling out cash to pay must both be written as concrete actions he takes—writing a single line like "he was exposed" is not enough. Viewing the workstation is the next step, not yet rented; the new stopped watch only opens her master's thread without answering the old case yet. Each of the three chapters has its own task and progress, with no need to force three rounds of humiliation.

## How Chapter 2 Picks Up the Opening: Delivering Real Outcomes After Verification

Continuing Xu Qinghe's watch-repair shop premise. The inspection record notes water ingress, and the photo is also on hand; the square-ring fragment can only prompt her to investigate further. Below, Chapter 2 expands the verification and quote, keeping the repair process brief while retaining acceptance inspection and payment. Eighty yuan is the quote chosen for this example.

> As soon as Boss Liu stepped through the door, his knuckles rapped on the glass counter: "My watch was fine when I dropped it off—how did water get inside in your hands?"
>
> Xu Qinghe slid the intake photo and inspection record side by side across the counter, tapping the water-ingress location noted on the record with the tip of her tweezers: "It was already there at intake. Look at this photo—repairs hadn't even started yet."
>
> He picked up the record and compared it against the photo, then pulled his hand back from the counter: "Fine, that part isn't on your repair. How much to fix it?"
>
> "Eighty." She wrote the number beneath the record and pushed the pen over with it.
>
> Boss Liu signed beside it: "Fix it." Only then did she enter the watch onto the pending-repair sheet.
>
> Once the watch was repaired, Boss Liu held it to his ear and listened for a while, found nothing to fault, and counted out eighty yuan onto the counter. Xu Qinghe crossed the item off the sheet and slipped the cash into the envelope for moving the shop. Six customer watches remained on the counter.

Verifying, withdrawing the claim, accepting the quote, and repairing for payment are four distinct outcomes. How the dispute shifts deserves detailed writing; the repair process can be summarized, while inspection and where the fee goes should stay. If you only write "he got slapped in the face," that entire chain of outcomes disappears.

Chapter 3 follows up with viewing the workstation and taking in the new stopped watch, counting this labor fee toward the relocation budget. The square ring still cannot identify who damaged the watch, her master's past still needs investigating, and viewing the workstation does not mean it is rented yet. Relationship-driven stories can pay off with understanding, cooperation, or a decision—choose the payoff according to what your book promises.

## Golden Three Chapters Self-Check Scorecard (Copyable)

This self-check table is for spotting where an opening feels hollow, not for predicting post-launch performance; mark each item as "Clear / Needs Work / N/A".

| Dimension | Check & Warning Signal |
|---|---|
| Opening Hook | **Check**: Is there a concrete matter readers want to keep following? **Signal**: Only reciting info, with an unclear task |
| Protagonist | **Check**: Situation, what they care about, and immediate choice; **Signal**: Only slapping on personality labels |
| Payoff | **Check**: Has the progress promised to readers actually landed? **Signal**: Claiming victory in words while the situation hasn't changed |
| Information | **Check**: Can readers understand the matter right in front of them? **Signal**: New terminology drowns out action |
| Chapter Ending | **Check**: What does the next chapter pick up? **Signal**: Empty teasers that leave no concrete question |
| Bystander Reactions | **Check**: What did the people present actually do? **Signal**: Everyone gasps in unison, nobody makes a real move |

Slow-burn or slice-of-life works deliver payoff through emotion, relationships, and shifts in understanding—no need to force "two thrill beats." When revising, fix the spot that hurts your book's core appeal the most first.

## Editor Says Opening Pacing Is Too Slow? Check These Spots First

First look at which paragraphs are doing no work, rather than hacking away at word count the moment you hear "slow." Check down the line for changes in goals, information, relationships, stakes/risks, identity, leverage/chips, and emotion; pauses that hold up atmosphere or a character's state of mind should be kept in line with your book's style.

| Symptom | Diagnosis & Fix |
|---|---|
| Main plot stays unclear | **Check first**: Why is the character staying here? **Fix**: Reveal the immediate task and the stakes wagered on it as early as possible |
| Dialogue spins in circles | **Check first**: Did the situation change after all the back-and-forth talk? **Fix**: Cut repetitive pleasantries; keep only probing, clashes, and relationship shifts |
| Background drowns out main business | **Check first**: Does all of this worldbuilding really need to be explained right now? **Fix**: Keep only the few lines needed to understand the immediate conflict |
| Flashbacks won't stop | **Check first**: Did this past event change the character's present judgment? **Fix**: Keep the one stroke that matters and compress the rest |
| Winning or losing is all talk | **Check first**: Did the person calling the shots or paying up actually take a stance? **Fix**: Add the involved party's concrete action and delivered outcome |

A chapter title can highlight the theme, but it cannot rescue main text that marches in place. If the conflict is thin, add character choices and consequences—do not shoehorn in sudden disasters just to hit a fixed word-count mark.

## Common Web Novel Opening Mistakes & Fixes

- Golden finger drops from the sky to save the day: Show its basic usage and limits ahead of time; an ordinary person's craft or professional know-how can equally serve as the hook.
- Character setup relies entirely on narrator praise: Test it inside an immediate difficulty; a character feeling afraid or hesitating does not lose points, as long as their motives and trade-offs are clear.
- POV and timeline jump erratically: Set the narrative perspective and scene-cut points cleanly; there is no need to blanket-ban flashbacks or multi-POV.
- Blurb and main text feel like two different books: Check whether the title, blurb, and first three chapters deliver the same flavor, and do not twist character decisions just to hit word-count milestones.

Opening techniques are only a toolbox—within the same genre, swapping in a different character situation, task, and order of revealing cards lets you avoid cookie-cutter opening templates.

# Web Novel Chapter-Ending Techniques: How to Write and Choose Among 13 Chapter-Ending Hooks

Web novel chapter-ending breaks leave a lingering thought at the end of a chapter that makes readers want to turn to the next page. Oh Story organizes 13 types of chapter-ending hooks and 7 types of chapter-opening methods, chosen according to the chapter's function, emotion, and how the next chapter picks up: a high-pressure chapter can stop on an urgent crisis, while a slice-of-life chapter only needs a small relationship step or quiet concern—no need to max out every single chapter, and even less need to rotate through tricks in a fixed queue.

## What Does Web Novel Chapter-Ending Mean?

Web novel chapter-ending refers to an author leaving an unanswered action, question, or emotional anticipation at the end of a chapter so readers want to keep reading the next chapter. The closing that stops there is a chapter-ending hook; together with chapter-opening hooks and small mid-paragraph suspense beats, they are collectively called hooks.

Oh Story's suspense reference uses a bomb analogy: if a bomb suddenly explodes, readers get startled and move on—that is surprise; if they hear the timer ticking without knowing which second it will blow, readers keep staring at it—that is suspense. What a chapter ending must leave behind is that still-ticking timer. Oh Story's bottom line is: after reading the final sentence of every chapter, the reader still wants to turn the page.

## What Chapter-Ending Hooks Exist? 13 Types & Example Sentences

The table below lists the 13 chapter-ending hook types shared by Oh Story's long-form and short-form writing references; all example sentences are original.

| Type | Technique & Original Example |
|---|---|
| 1. Sudden Revelation | **Technique:** Drop a piece of information that rewrites the situation **Original Example:** The person signing for that shipment on the warehouse surveillance camera was wearing her old raincoat. |
| 2. Urgent Crisis | **Technique:** Leave a threat that the next chapter cannot avoid answering **Original Example:** River water had already risen past the third step, and the boat still wasn't fixed. |
| 3. Unfinished Action | **Technique:** An action halfway done is interrupted by a new variable **Original Example:** She reached out to press the doorbell; before her fingertip touched it, the bell rang first. |
| 4. Identity Reversal | **Technique:** Someone's true identity does not match what readers assumed **Original Example:** The newly arrived bookkeeper handed over his letter of recommendation; the seal at the bottom belonged to the household whose property was confiscated three years ago. |
| 5. Dilemma Choice | **Technique:** Force a character to pick between two bad options **Original Example:** Hand over the ledger, and Master lives; refuse, and not a single shop on this street will survive. |
| 6. Mysterious Object | **Technique:** Reveal an important item whose meaning is unclear **Original Example:** Tucked beneath the lunchbox was a square of red cloth wrapped around a baby tooth, with her birthday embroidered on the corner. |
| 7. Countdown | **Technique:** Make time visibly insufficient **Original Example:** Ten minutes left before the exam bell, and his admission ticket had been left in the taxi. |
| 8. Promise / Threat | **Technique:** Someone openly declares what they are about to do next **Original Example:** "Before daybreak, I'm selling this shop—along with you." |
| 9. Bizarre Disappearance | **Technique:** Write a disappearance impossible by ordinary logic **Original Example:** Only a single line of footprints in the snow led into the woodshed; inside the woodshed, there was no one. |
| 10. Hidden Meaning | **Technique:** A flaw hidden inside an ordinary remark **Original Example:** Her cousin laughed over the phone: "Give my regards to that osmanthus tree downstairs." The osmanthus had only been planted yesterday. |
| 11. Imagery Hook | **Technique:** A recurring image suddenly changes **Original Example:** That window across the street that stayed lit until ten every night went dark at eight today. |
| 12. Echo Hook | **Technique:** Repeat the chapter's opening line at the end while altering a key detail **Original Example:** Opening: "He could walk this street with his eyes closed." Ending: "He could walk this street with his eyes closed. Tonight he opened his eyes and stopped at the intersection." |
| 13. Blank-Space Hook | **Technique:** Write only the character's reaction without explaining what happened **Original Example:** After hanging up that call, she turned off the stove and went out without locking the door. |

## What Chapter-Opening Hooks Exist? 7 Ways to Open a Chapter

A chapter-ending hook delivers readers to the next chapter, and the chapter opening must catch them immediately. Oh Story has 7 chapter-opening hook types:

| Type | Technique & Original Example |
|---|---|
| 1. Suspense-Dialogue Opening | **Technique:** Open directly with dialogue full of subtext **Original Example:** "Did you see him?" "Saw him." "Did he recognize you?" |
| 2. Flash-Forward Fragment | **Technique:** Give a fragment of the outcome first, then return to normal narration **Original Example:** Sirens stopped downstairs at three in the morning. Twelve hours earlier, she had still been arguing with the landlord over a utility bill. |
| 3. Countdown Opening | **Technique:** Establish urgency from the very first sentence **Original Example:** Forty hours left until the demolition crew entered the village. |
| 4. Mysterious Monologue | **Technique:** Strange inner thoughts in first person **Original Example:** The person knocking that day knocked nine times in total—I counted—yet I only opened the door once. |
| 5. Contrast Scene | **Technique:** Place two starkly opposite scenes side by side **Original Example:** In the front courtyard, a birthday banquet was set and the opera stage had just struck its gong. By the back-courtyard well, someone was washing a muddy iron shovel. |
| 6. Unfinished-Action Opening | **Technique:** The opening action is cut off halfway through **Original Example:** He had just torn open one corner of the envelope when someone outside the window called his childhood nickname. |
| 7. Imagery Foreshadowing | **Technique:** Use environmental imagery to hint that trouble is coming **Original Example:** A whole row of paper lanterns drifted down the river, not a single one lit. After counting them, she got up to close the window. |

Which type to pick depends on how you want the chapter to open: for direct impact, use an Unfinished-Action Opening or Countdown Opening; to create curiosity, use Suspense Dialogue, a Flash-Forward Fragment, or a Mysterious Monologue; to grab attention through contrast, use a Contrast Scene or Imagery Foreshadowing.

Compare the chapter opening side by side with the expectation left by the previous chapter: is it continuing action, verifying information, or carrying forward emotion? Both long-form and short-form judge by the book's needs rather than treating "first hundred characters" or "no weather openings" as universal rules. For how to open Chapter 1 of the entire book, see [How to Write a Novel Opening](opening_and_hooks.md).

## How to Write Chapter-Ending Hooks: Choosing by Chapter Emotion and Story Stage

First look at what this chapter actually finished writing, then think about what the next chapter needs to pick up, and finally pick the hook. The table below is only a selection reference—do not apply it rigidly by genre or stage.

| Chapter Responsibility | Landing Point & Handoff |
|---|---|
| Anomaly Discovered | **Candidate Landing Points:** Object, shift in information **What Next Chapter Should Do:** Verify, rule out, or act on it |
| Pressure Incoming | **Candidate Landing Points:** Countdown, crisis **What Next Chapter Should Do:** Respond to that pressure without switching to a different problem |
| Victory Completed | **Candidate Landing Points:** New responsibility, relationship, or aftertaste **What Next Chapter Should Do:** Acknowledge the payoff already earned |
| Relationship Shift | **Candidate Landing Points:** Request, choice, emotion **What Next Chapter Should Do:** Let the other party respond or advance the relationship |
| Low-Pressure Daily Life | **Candidate Landing Points:** Small goal, lingering emotion **What Next Chapter Should Do:** Advance naturally without forcing artificial secrets |
| Book Finale | **Candidate Landing Points:** Fulfillment and echo **What Next Chapter Should Do:** Allow closure without opening new suspense |

When outlining, watch by default for ups and downs in hook intensity and whether two adjacent chapters clash by repeating the same type: if several chapters in a row use the same ending and serve the same function, switch it up; if the conditions of the same timed crisis keep changing, using countdowns back-to-back is completely valid. Never throw away a newly established goal just for the sake of variety.

A hook stops as soon as the suspense is clearly stated. If the next chapter dodges it without picking it up—only muttering "there are still secrets ahead"—no amount of variety will keep it from spinning its wheels.

## Chapter-Ending Hook Example: Rewriting an AI-Style Ending into a Proper Cliffhanger

Original setting: A ferryman has been missing for a year, and his daughter has taken over the ferry. The previous chapter's countdown established that flood season is three days away; this chapter has already checked her father's ledger and found an advance ferry fee, though she does not yet know its purpose. Below are two rewrites, both using only facts already introduced in this chapter.

**Vague Teaser**:

> She closed the ledger, finally understanding her father's painstaking intentions. Little did she know, this ledger would drag her into a massive vortex.

If the entire book stays tight to the daughter's limited POV, she has not yet figured out her father's intentions at this moment, so the narrator cannot declare "finally understanding" on her behalf; "a massive vortex" is also hollow and leaves readers unsure what to wait for in the next chapter.

**Candidate 1: Concrete Object**. First write her flipping to an inserted page, pulling out a ferry ticket, and reading "father's name" and "the third day of next month," then stop at:

> She placed the ferry ticket beside that advance payment. The passenger was her father, yet the date was the third day of next month.

In the next chapter, she first checks the booking record and issuer of this ticket; a future date only shows someone booked a ticket—it cannot prove her father is alive, much less say where he is.

**Candidate 2: Action with a Question**. This chapter has already shown her "Old East-Bank Pier" written beside the advance payment, and established that the boat can cross during the day, then stop at:

> She circled "Old East-Bank Pier" and wrapped up the ledger. First thing tomorrow morning, she would go there and ask.

The next chapter writes her going ashore to ask around, getting one solid lead or hitting a new obstacle. This version puts the next action right out in the open without making the character play dumb with readers after already seeing the place name. Pick one of the two rewrites—do not cram both into the chapter ending; the missing-person main plot can be investigated slowly, as long as each step pushes forward one inch.

## Common Chapter-Ending Hook Errors: How to Fix AI-Style Chapter Endings

First look at what information the ending lands on and whether it does any work, rather than labeling something "AI tone" based on a single sentence pattern alone. In a tight limited-POV story, having the narrator jump out to spoil later events crosses a line; if the book naturally uses omniscient narration, appropriate foreshadowing is fine—just check whether it is concrete and free of circular filler.

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Character hasn't figured out the cause yet, narrator declares "understood" | Keep the unknown intact; write only judgments deducible from immediate facts |
| "Bigger vortex" doesn't tell readers what to wait for | Replace with one verifiable item or concrete next action |
| Re-summarizing the change that was just acted out | Delete the recap; one line of reflection fitting the character's mood may stay |
| Maxing out the ending of every single chapter | Leave breathing room and aftertaste according to the chapter's tension |
| Continuing to explain after throwing out the hook | Stop writing on the exact sentence where the suspense is clearest |
| Ignoring the hook in the next chapter to write something else | First pick up the thread or goal left by the previous chapter |

Stopping on an action, dialogue, a thought in the character's head, or a narrator's pointed remark all work as long as they fit your book's style—there is no need to force every insight and emotion into tiny hand-and-eye gestures. For removing AI flavor across an entire chapter, see [Removing AI Flavor](deslop_expression.md).

## How the Next Chapter Picks Up: Actually Paying Off the Hook Instead of Repeating "More Secrets Ahead"

Continuing with Candidate 1 above, without cramming Candidate 2 into this version. To write the handoff, the author selects this set of original follow-up facts: the ticket has a serial number, the town ticket office can look up current bookings by serial number, and yesterday's clerk on duty is right at the counter. These are details decided by the author for the new chapter and could not be deduced from her father's name or the future date alone.

> The next day, as soon as the ticket office opened, she set the ticket on the counter: "Was this ticket issued here?"
>
> The clerk on duty flipped to the log page by the serial number and pointed it out to her: "Issued yesterday, booked for the third day of next month."
>
> "Yesterday? Who came to book it?"
>
> "Booked by phone. The name was given over the phone—I didn't see anyone."
>
> The passenger line also bore her father's name. Her father had been missing for a year, and yesterday someone booked a ticket under that name; those two facts still couldn't be pieced together into "he's back."
>
> "Who answered the phone?"
>
> "I did," the clerk said. "Four-twenty in the afternoon."
>
> She jotted the time in the margin of the ledger and took back the ferry ticket: "I'll check that phone call first. Leave the booking as it is—don't change it."
>
> The clerk nodded. She closed the ledger and left with the ticket.

This handoff verifies the serial number, issue time, and phone booking, narrowing what needs investigating next to a single question: who gave that name. Her father still hasn't been found, nor is it proven that the caller was him. The clerk's words are on-site testimony and the log page is immediate evidence, yet neither yields the whole truth.

Checking the phone call is her next plan; this passage has not yet traced the caller. If the prose only dropped a line like "she didn't know a bigger secret was waiting," this whole trip to verify the ticket would have been wasted. The long missing-person thread can be uncovered gradually, while the immediate question raised must first get an echo: advancing one small step per inquiry is enough, without needing to unmask the mastermind or trigger a catastrophic crisis every time.
