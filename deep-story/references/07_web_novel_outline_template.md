# How to Write a Web Novel Outline: Long-Form Novel Outline Template & Volume Examples

Do not write a web novel outline straight through from Chapter 1 onward. First define in one sentence what the protagonist must accomplish, then decide the ending and each volume's climax, and work backward to determine the situation that must change in every volume; only at the end should you arrange adjustable scenes. The book's overall direction should stay stable, volume-level paths can be revised, and specific dialogue and small scenes should be left until writing time.

## Which Layer of a Long-Form Outline Should You Write First?

An outline is meant to anchor the direction of a long-form work, not to pre-write the main text. It is generally divided into three layers—whatever names you use are up to your habit:

| Layer | Purpose & Scope of Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Book-Wide Spine | What the protagonist must ultimately accomplish and why it has to be them; sets the overarching direction. When changing it midway, first check against already written chapters and reader expectations. |
| Volume Route | How far this volume pushes the situation and how the volume-end climax plays out; specific paths can be adjusted, but do not overturn previously established causes. |
| Scene Memos | Which scene, clue, or dialogue you plan to use to cross a beat; revise as you write. Ideas not yet written into the main text do not count as established facts. |

This has a different division of labor from the [Detailed Chapter Outline Template](08_novel_chapter_outline_template.md): the book-wide outline governs the big direction, while the detailed outline governs how to write the next few chapters. This article recommends deciding the climax first and working backward, though you can also start by exploring characters or trial-writing the opening and then converge on the endpoint once the flow feels right; do not force yourself to invent unused secrets, abilities, or subplots just to fill out a form.

## Copyable Long-Form Novel Outline Template

Fill in the following order; you do not have to fill out every subplot all at once:

> **Core Selling Point**: What unique experience are readers following ongoing updates for?
>
> **Protagonist's Starting Point**: What do they currently lack, what constrains them, and what are they most afraid of losing?
>
> **Book-Wide Goal**: One concrete thing they must ultimately accomplish whose success or failure is clear.
>
> **Endgame Choice**: What binary choice must they make at the book's climax? How does this choice show they are different from the opening?
>
> **Ending State**: Where do the external situation, core relationships, and the protagonist's own mindset each land at the end?
>
> **Volume Card**: Volume goal | Major obstacle | New strategy | Climax showdown | Partial payoff | Remaining pressure.
>
> **Cross-Volume Arcs**: How the main plot escalates; which relationship or secret advances in which volume and is revealed in which volume.
>
> **Current Writing Window**: Only detail the goal, resistance, outcome, and chapter-end hook for the next few chapters.

Write the endgame choice and each volume's climax first, then work backward to the characters, abilities, relationships, and evidence that need to be established in advance. That way foreshadowing comes from the needs of the ending, avoiding stuffing things in ad hoc wherever you happen to be writing.

## Original Volume Example: *The Tide Restorer* (《潮汐修复师》)

**Original Setting *The Tide Restorer* (《潮汐修复师》)**: Xu Cheng can hear the sounds from before and after a malfunction in old machinery she has touched, but cannot directly know who swapped a part or why. A major tide is approaching the coastal city, and she wants to repair the main sluice gate and uncover the chain of part swaps behind the consecutive failures. The maintenance team controls construction eligibility, spare parts, and gate-closure scheduling; the archivist provides registered records; the municipal council decides which maintenance method to adopt. Xu Cheng cannot obtain any of these authorities through her ability alone.

Below is the four-volume plan to be written. First fix the final action that must be completed, then distribute the prerequisites into earlier volumes:

| Volume | Goal, Climax & Handoff to Next Volume |
|---|---|
| Salt Bay Old Gate | Squeeze into the maintenance team. Xu Cheng points out an anomaly in a branch gate, and the maintenance team inspects it before agreeing to let her work on it; once the branch gate is repaired, the team leader nods to let her into follow-up projects. However, the serial numbers on the removed parts don't match the registry, pointing clues toward the pumping station records. |
| South City Pumping Station | Find out how the parts were swapped. She and the archivist lay out three records side by side—checkout, procurement, and actual assembly—and ask the maintenance team to double-check, proving this batch of parts was not installed per the records; why they were swapped this way remains uncertain. The contractor withdraws the earlier recommendation, while the old blueprint of the main gate is missing a page; the next volume must track down its source. |
| Ebb-Tide Archives | Recover the missing page and the handling records from back then. Her companion confesses they had the missing page all along and hands it to the archivist for filing; Xu Cheng only then learns her father took on the repair responsibility back then under a temporary defense arrangement, which led to him being misunderstood, yet pursuing the investigation further still lacks complete records. The two agree to cross-check the materials together going forward; whether trust can return depends on how they act later. Worse, the major tide arrives early, tightening the construction window. |
| Night of Sealing the Tide | The maintenance team prepares matching parts and approves the gate-closure window; Xu Cheng chooses to go down for emergency repairs first and leave the complete records for public verification after the meeting. Only after repairs, gate testing, and evacuation are all finished does the council hear her explanation and vote to adopt a method of making maintenance records public; the ending lands on the actual registration of the next scheduled inspection. Submitting the materials does not mean all responsibility is settled. |

Her father's past experience, the replacement parts, the gate-closure schedule, and who has the authority to approve the new maintenance method must all be laid down in advance across earlier volumes. Hearing mechanical anomalies is only the pretext to investigate clues; it cannot replace assembly ledgers, on-site verification, and other people's stances. The endgame brings her craft, verified records, and accumulated cooperative relationships together in one place—never conjure authority or resources out of thin air in the final scene.

For now, you only need to flesh out Volume 1's "apply to participate — verify misjudgment — disassemble and discover — branch gate emergency repair"; leave later dialogue, on-site action, and minor twists until you write that volume's detailed outline, instead of filling out Volume 4 right now.

## From Blank Page to Writable: Working Backward from the Ending for Key Plot Points

First set the protagonist's starting point and endpoint to widen the before-and-after contrast; then think through what trade-off they must make in the book's biggest conflict, rather than writing a vague "ultimate showdown." Next, work backward: what gives them the leverage to make that decision? The skills, connections, and hard evidence they currently lack get broken down into milestones to capture in earlier volumes.

Plan each volume the same way: set the volume-end climax first, then give the start of the volume a reachable stage goal, and place hard obstacles in the middle that force the protagonist to change tactics. Keep a subplot only if it helps the main plot, adds cost to the protagonist, or merges back into the main plot at the end. If you drift off course mid-writing, first check whether the current action still advances the volume's goal, instead of reflexively scrapping and rewriting the entire volume.

## Common Long-Form Outline Failures & Fixes

| Problem | Diagnosis & Fix |
|---|---|
| Treating "leveling up / getting stronger" as the main plot | Why it stalls: Getting stronger is only a means, with no visible endpoint; Fix: Clearly write "which specific task must be accomplished after getting stronger." |
| Pushing forward from the opening wherever the pen leads | Why it stalls: Reaching a late-stage climax only to realize setup is missing; Fix: Decide the climax choice first, then work backward for prerequisites. |
| Every volume just swaps maps and starts over | Why it stalls: After one round, everything resets to zero with no accumulation; Fix: Make the outcome of the previous volume trigger the new trouble of the next volume. |
| Many subplots that never touch each other | Why it stalls: Word count grows while the main plot stands still; Fix: Specify whether each subplot ultimately helps, blocks, or rewrites the main plot. |
| Writing the outline like full-text scenes | Why it stalls: Great ideas pop up while drafting the main text, but there's no room to fit them in; Fix: Lock down the big direction and leave maneuvering room for specific scenes. |

To judge whether an outline is sufficient, simply check if you can answer: what is being pursued in the next stage, who is blocking it, what will be lost upon failure, and what will the climax change.
